## Capital Risk
Summary
Capital risk is the risk that an institution will not have sufficient capital to absorb unexpected losses and continue operations
Regulators require institutions to maintain minimum capital ratios as a buffer against asset deterioration and unexpected losses
Capital adequacy is measured through a series of risk-weighted ratios that compare eligible capital to risk-adjusted asset exposures
An institution with strong capital levels can absorb stress scenarios across its balance sheet without compromising operations or solvency
FinPro recommends institutions maintain capital well above minimums to provide flexibility for growth, acquisitions, and stress events
Capital planning must account for both current risk-weighted asset levels and the likely trajectory of the balance sheet under baseline and stress scenarios
Dividend and retained earnings policies directly influence capital accumulation and should be aligned with the institution's risk appetite
Definition
Capital risk is the risk that a financial institution will not maintain sufficient capital to absorb unexpected losses, support its risk profile, and meet regulatory requirements at all times. Capital serves as the institution's primary financial cushion — the residual value available to absorb losses before deposits and debt are impaired. Regulatory capital frameworks measure capital adequacy through a series of ratios that compare eligible capital components to risk-weighted assets, with minimum requirements established by bank regulators. An institution experiencing capital erosion may face regulatory constraints on growth, dividend payments, and acquisitions, and in severe cases may face mandatory corrective action under prompt corrective action (PCA) frameworks. Capital risk management therefore encompasses not only meeting current minimums but also projecting capital adequacy forward under various scenarios to ensure resilience across the institution's strategic time horizon.
Significance
Capital adequacy is fundamental to regulatory compliance, stakeholder confidence, and the institution's long-term viability. Institutions operating near minimum capital thresholds have limited flexibility to absorb losses from credit deterioration, interest rate shocks, operational failures, or liquidity stress. Strong capital positions support access to funding markets, correspondent banking relationships, and favorable regulatory treatment.
Near Term Strategies
Review capital ratios quarterly against regulatory minimums and institution-specific targets
Conduct forward-looking capital stress tests under baseline and adverse scenarios
Manage dividend payout ratios to align with capital accretion targets
Evaluate risk-weighted asset density and restructure balance sheet to improve capital efficiency where appropriate
Establish board-approved capital minimums above regulatory floors as a management buffer

## Credit Risk
Summary
Credit risk is the risk that a borrower or counterparty will fail to fulfill contractual obligations, resulting in a financial loss
It is the most fundamental and typically the largest source of risk for most financial institutions
Credit risk spans the full loan portfolio, investment portfolio, and off-balance-sheet positions such as unfunded commitments
Loan portfolio
Investment portfolio
Off-balance-sheet commitments and contingencies
Effective credit risk management requires robust underwriting standards, portfolio monitoring, concentration limits, and allowance adequacy
Loan-to-value ratios, debt service coverage, and borrower financial strength are key underwriting inputs that directly affect credit quality
FinPro recommends institutions set explicit policy limits on loan concentrations by sector, geography, and collateral type
The allowance for credit losses must be calibrated to current expected credit losses across all loan categories
Definition
Credit risk is the possibility that a borrower, issuer, or counterparty will fail to perform their financial obligations as agreed, resulting in a loss of principal, accrued interest, or other contractual payments. For lending institutions, credit risk is the dominant source of risk and is embedded in every loan originated, every investment purchased, and every off-balance-sheet commitment made. The magnitude of credit risk depends on the probability of default, the exposure at the time of default, and the loss severity after recovery actions are taken. Credit risk management encompasses the full cycle from underwriting and approval through ongoing monitoring, portfolio management, problem asset resolution, and charge-off. Regulatory expectations include sound credit underwriting policies, periodic loan review, appropriate allowance methodology, and board oversight of credit risk appetite and concentrations.
Significance
Losses from credit risk directly impair the capital and earnings of a financial institution. Poor credit quality can cascade into liquidity pressures as problem assets restrict borrowing capacity and erode depositor confidence. Strong credit risk management protects balance sheet integrity, supports earnings stability, and enables the institution to weather economic downturns without requiring external capital support.
Near Term Strategies
Strengthen underwriting standards and consistently apply them across all loan categories
Set and enforce concentration limits by loan type, industry, geography, and borrower
Perform timely and objective loan reviews to identify deteriorating credits early
Ensure the allowance for credit losses is sufficient under CECL methodology
Review and stress test collateral valuations, especially for commercial real estate portfolios

## Market Risk
Summary
Market risk is the risk of financial loss resulting from adverse changes in market prices, rates, or indices
For financial institutions, market risk is primarily driven by changes in interest rates, equity prices, and credit spreads
The investment portfolio, trading positions, and mortgage pipeline are the primary market risk exposures for most community banks
Changes in market interest rates affect both the economic value of equity and net interest income across the institution's balance sheet
Market risk must be assessed over both short-term income horizons and long-term economic value perspectives
Effective market risk management includes mark-to-market monitoring, duration analysis, and scenario analysis
Definition
Market risk is the risk that the value of a financial institution's assets, liabilities, or off-balance-sheet positions will decline due to adverse movements in market prices, interest rates, foreign exchange rates, or other market variables. For most community and regional banks, market risk is primarily manifested through interest rate risk — the sensitivity of net interest income and economic value of equity to changes in the level and shape of the interest rate yield curve. Market risk also encompasses the risk of unrealized losses in the available-for-sale investment securities portfolio, which flow directly through other comprehensive income and can affect regulatory capital ratios. Effective market risk management requires an integrated Asset/Liability Management (ALM) framework that monitors exposure across both income and capital dimensions.
Significance
Market risk exposures can rapidly erode both reported earnings and capital under adverse market conditions. Rising interest rates compress net interest margins on fixed-rate assets while increasing funding costs. Falling rates compress yields and extend asset durations. Investment portfolio losses under rate stress can impair capital and restrict strategic flexibility. Institutions must proactively manage their interest rate risk position within board-approved sensitivity limits.
Near Term Strategies
Perform quarterly interest rate risk sensitivity analysis across multiple rate scenarios
Review asset and liability repricing gaps and manage within policy limits
Monitor unrealized gains and losses in the investment securities portfolio relative to capital
Develop and maintain a comprehensive ALM policy reviewed annually by the board
Evaluate the use of interest rate derivatives to hedge significant rate exposures where appropriate

## Interest Rate Risk
Summary
Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in market interest rates will adversely affect net interest income or the economic value of equity
It is the dominant form of market risk for most financial institutions
Measured across two primary dimensions: earnings at risk (short-term income perspective) and economic value of equity (long-term capital perspective)
Key drivers include repricing imbalances between assets and liabilities, embedded optionality, and yield curve shape
Policy limits should be set for both earnings at risk and economic value of equity under multiple rate shock scenarios
Definition
Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in market interest rates will adversely impact a financial institution's net interest income, net interest margin, or economic value of equity. It arises from mismatches between the interest rate sensitivity characteristics of assets and liabilities — specifically from differences in timing of repricings, maturities, and embedded options such as prepayment rights on loans and call features on investment securities. Financial regulators expect institutions to measure, monitor, and control interest rate risk within board-approved policy limits, with quantified exposure at multiple interest rate scenarios. The standard measurement framework includes basis point shocks (typically ±100, ±200, ±300, ±400 bps) applied to the current yield curve and evaluated for impact on both projected 12-month net interest income and the present value of future cash flows (economic value of equity).
Significance
Interest rate risk is the primary driver of earnings volatility for most financial institutions. Poorly managed rate exposure can result in compressed net interest margins as rates shift, capital impairment through investment portfolio losses, and reduced institutional flexibility during economic transitions. Board and management oversight of interest rate risk is a regulatory priority, and institutions with outsized exposures face heightened supervisory scrutiny.
